# Google Flights Usability Test: Task Specifications

This document defines **three usability testing tasks** designed to evaluate whether frequent travelers can effectively use **Google Flights** to plan their trips. The tasks range systematically from easy to challenging, and are formulated to be realistic, verifiable, and non-leading.

---

## 1. Project Title
**Usability Test Plan: Evaluating Trip Planning Effectiveness on Google Flights**

---

## 2. Task 1: Basic Round-Trip Search (Easy)

### Task Instruction Wording:
> *"You are planning a weekend trip to Chicago. You want to leave New York City (any airport) on Friday evening, October 16, 2026, and return on Sunday afternoon, October 18, 2026. Find the cheapest available direct round-trip flight, and write down the total fare price and the airline operating the flight."*

### Why this is a good task:
* **Realistic:** Weekend leisure trips are one of the most common user behaviors on travel booking websites.
* **Verifiable:** The researcher can verify the result by checking if the participant correctly identifies the specific cheapest direct flight price and airline matching those exact dates.
* **Non-Leading:** It does not instruct the user where to click, how to filter, or which airport codes (e.g., LGA, JFK, EWR, ORD, MDW) to type. It simply gives the criteria (New York to Chicago, direct flight, specific dates, cheapest rate).

---

## 3. Task 2: Multi-City Itinerary with Specific Schedule Constraints (Medium)

### Task Instruction Wording:
> *"You are planning a business trip where you need to visit two different cities. You need to fly from San Francisco (SFO) to Seattle (SEA) on Monday morning, November 9, 2026. After your meeting, you need to fly from Seattle (SEA) to Boston (BOS) on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, arriving in Boston before 6:00 PM. Finally, you will return from Boston (BOS) back to San Francisco (SFO) on Friday, November 13, 2026. Find the flight itinerary that minimizes total travel costs while ensuring you arrive in Boston on time. Note down the total cost, the names of the airlines for each leg, and your arrival time in Boston."*

### Why this is a good task:
* **Realistic:** Business travelers frequently have to manage multi-city stops with rigid meeting times and corporate budget limits.
* **Verifiable:** The researcher can easily verify whether the chosen flight itinerary meets the arrival deadline (before 6:00 PM in Boston on Nov 11) and represents the lowest cost combination matching the dates.
* **Non-Leading:** It requires the user to discover and use the "Multi-city" search option instead of default round-trip tabs, and apply time filters on the Seattle-Boston leg without explicit instructions (e.g., it doesn't say *"click the time filter slider"*).

---

## 4. Task 3: Group Booking with Complex Accommodations (Challenging)

### Task Instruction Wording:
> *"You are booking a vacation to Tokyo, Japan for a family of three: two adults and one infant (under 2 years old) who will sit on an adult's lap. You want to depart from Los Angeles (LAX) on Friday, December 11, 2026, and return on Sunday, December 27, 2026. One of the adults has a medical condition that requires a lie-flat seat for the long flight, so you want to book Business Class for the trip, but you want to check if the price would be significantly cheaper if you booked Premium Economy instead. Additionally, you want to bring two checked bags in total. Compare the prices for these two cabin classes, determine the cheapest airline for each, and locate the baggage policy details to confirm if checked bags are included in your ticket price. Write down your findings."*

### Why this is a good task:
* **Realistic:** Families traveling long-distance often balance complex comfort requirements (infants, cabin class upgrades for medical or comfort reasons) against baggage allowance policies.
* **Verifiable:** The participant must produce two distinct prices (Business vs. Premium Economy) for a 2-adult-1-infant booking on those dates, name the cheapest airlines, and verify the baggage rules.
* **Non-Leading:** It forces the user to navigate advanced drop-downs (passenger count adjustments, infant-on-lap options, cabin class switches), compare side-by-side search results, and dig into deep policy links for baggage information, all without any interface guidance.
